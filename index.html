Prototype: creating an Envoy workplace map with a phone camera

Show, not tell. A small experiment with Envoy Workplace Maps.

What if you could set up a workplace map by walking the floor with your phone, with no floor plan and no drawing?

Built in an afternoon with AI coding tools, as a thought exercise and for fun. I have no inside knowledge of Envoy's constraints or priorities, so plenty here is assumption.

The problem

While exploring Envoy, I spent time in Setting up Workplace Maps. I was impressed that it's self-serve. I had assumed map setup was white-glove work done by Envoy's team. Three steps stood out as slow:

1. You need a floor plan to start. Not every workplace has a current one.

2. Rooms are drawn by hand, corner by corner.

3. Everything else is placed one at a time: desks, safety equipment, amenities. This grows as customers' needs do, for example specialised equipment in a pharma lab.

The idea

How might Envoy shorten the time from "create floor" to a published map, without the result being any worse than one made by hand?

Capture the space with a phone. One walk-through gives you both the layout and what's in it.

What I built

Two ways to start. On desktop, upload a floor plan or capture the floor with the mobile app.

Walk the floor and the plan draws itself. No floor plan needed.

Things in the space are picked up and grouped.

Rooms are detected, not drawn.

Review, edit and save.

This isn't a replacement for the existing map editor. It only shortens the first draft. Editing afterwards, especially on desktop, will still be needed.

Happy to demo it live. It needs an iPhone Pro, since it uses the LiDAR sensor.

Why it might matter

I don't see this winning more deals. It's a quality-of-life improvement for customers who have already committed, at one of their first interactions with the product: a possible "wow" moment in onboarding, and fewer support requests about map setup.

If it isn't obviously faster than today's flow, it's a bad idea.

If this were real, where I'd start

  1. Is this even a problem? If most customers can get a floor plan easily, a cheaper answer is AI that reads the existing plan and tags rooms and labels. That is easier to ship and may be more accurate. Capturing only wins if plans are missing, out of date, or don't show what's actually in the space. I'd ask Support and recently onboarded customers first.
  2. Who is it for? A 20-person startup and a multi-floor enterprise or defence customer have very different spaces and tolerances.
  3. Test it with customers who recently set up a map. Was it faster? Was it as accurate? Where did capturing a space feel unfamiliar?
  4. Fix what didn't work in the prototype.
    • Detection is narrow. Only monitors and fire extinguishers were reliable. AEDs and first aid kits failed in testing.
    • One room at a time works. Several rooms in one pass is shaky.
    • Untested: glass walls, large open floors, high ceilings, multiple floors. The sensor reaches about 5 m, and Apple's RoomPlan is built for rooms up to roughly 9 × 9 m.
  5. Privacy. Workplaces may not want their space recorded. Images should be used only to identify specific asset types, and not stored once the map is created.
More open questions
  • Engineering: fit with the existing map stack; editability afterwards; mobile performance; Android and phones without LiDAR; whether it's worth the cost.
  • Sales and support: a new interaction to teach; harder to support remotely because it happens on the customer's phone; possibly more support requests at first.
  • When is it good enough to ship?

Later

  • Re-capture to update. When the floor changes, walk it again and the map updates.
  • Less effort to capture. A handful of photos instead of a walk-through, or an agent doing it.
  • Zones. The same capture could define zones within a floor.
  • 3D. The capture produces a 3D model. I kept the output 2D on purpose: 3D needs a strong reason to exist, it's more to get wrong, and it's harder to render.

How I built it

I'm not an engineer. I built this with Claude Code in an afternoon, using Apple's RoomPlan for the layout and an open-source vision model (YOLO-World) to spot objects, tested at home on my own phone.
